Where does a quantum dot engine run best?

A single-level dot between a hot lead and a cold lead turns heat into electrical work. Maximize its power and the efficiency usually lands at half of Carnot. Hold the bias fixed and tune only the gate, and it climbs to almost all of Carnot. Move the sliders to see why.

Every hop is one real tunneling event, drawn from a stochastic simulation of the master equation, Eq. (2). The dot holds at most one electron. An electron absorbs heat Q1 = EQD to climb out of the hot lead. On entering the cold lead it sheds Q2 = EQD − Δμ. The difference, W = Δμ, is the work. Backward hops happen too; only the net flow does work. At 1×, one second of animation is six tunneling times 1/Γ.

Why the half-Carnot rule breaks

Van den Broeck's argument assumes the work-producing flux grows linearly with its force as you leave the stall point. Power is flux times force, so it is a parabola between zero force and the reversible point, and the peak sits halfway, at ηC/2.

With the bias fixed, the natural force is 1/EQD. Its zero, an infinitely high dot level, is not equilibrium: the current vanishes because the dot is simply out of reach, while the temperature difference is still there. Near that point the current dies like e−EQD/T2, an essential singularity rather than a straight line. If the flux instead rises like Xn+1, the peak moves to
